I am a man not of prudence, but principle, which is why I decided to dig deep into Thales of Miletus. As it turns out, he is an interesting character. The only thing we probably are sure about, in his story, is that he existed. Most other things, probable-improbable, it all is a bit iffy.

Thales of Miletus was born around 625 BC, came from a lineage of Phoenicians, and was the first of the seven sages. He is said to be the first to introduce the study of nature to the Greeks (again, take everything with a grain of salt), but most probably decided against writing. All of the information on Thales has been derived through succeeding texts. Here is a list of things he is purported to have done:

1. Gave useful advice before the destruction of Ionia.
2. Helped Croesus’ army to cross the River Halys.
3. Predicted the eclipse of May 28, 585 BC.
4. Being a reductionist, Thales alleged there to be some nature from which every other thing was derived. This nature, he came to believe, was water.
5. Believed the earth floated on water.
6. Held belief in an everlasting soul.
7. Discovered various geometrical truths.
8. Uncovered the constellation Ursa Minor (Little Bear).
9. Gave excellent political advice.
10. Measured the pyramids from their shadows.
11. Spoke a number of aphorisms.
12. Introduced logos in a populace fraught with myth.
13. Spoke the revered motto, “Know Thyself.”

In 548 BC, the man was watching a gymnastic contest when he was struck down by old age. Soon, the seventy-eight-year-old man had succumbed to heat, thirst, and weakness.

Thales was the first philosopher. If he did the things that he is said to have done, he was one of the sharper ones, too. But of course, it’s even dubious whether these texts refer to the same person or not. There are a number of Thales in history, and the one we imagine probably did not exist. It’s fascinating. How this legend-like character was the first philosopher. We know almost nothing of his philosophy, since none of his written works survived modernity. At least, writing that survived 2500 years.

Back when the only known thing about life was death, the only God-given right was to breathe, the only truth was rainfall, the only logic that of mythos and the gods, Thales of Miletus thought differently. Whether or not this man parted a river, whether or not this man deciphered the diameter, Thales of Miletus was an idolized thinker in his times. Diogenes Laertius was a renowned biographer who documented the early lives of several philosophers.

He is credited with having saved entire texts from the harsh hands of history; I’ll let Diogenes finish off with the writing on Thales’ tomb:

> His tomb is small, his fame is heaven-high.
