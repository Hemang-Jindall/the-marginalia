Anaximander is the purported pupil of the first Greek philosopher, Thales. Born in 610 BC, Anaximander is thought to have died around 540 BC. Much like Thales, he is etched so deep in antiquity that most dates, philosophies, and texts are unverified. He is known to have written a book titled On Nature and was one of the first human beings to produce a map—two, in fact, for one was of the world itself, and the other, much like the interests of his predecessor, of the stars in the sky.

But unlike Thales, Anaximander’s philosophy didn’t stray too far from the believed norms at that time. He believed the Earth to be flat, disc-shaped with depth—ideally, a cylinder. He also alleged the Earth to be the center of the universe. One of his lesser feats, which I believe to be one of his bigger ones, is his explanation of weather. When Greece was wrought with myths of anthropomorphic gods weeping, Anaximander was able to successfully hypothesize the cause of rain (to a certain extent).

He was actively observing the skies, trying to decipher wind, lightning, and thunder. Although not as robust as his philosophy on rain, these other aspects saw massive scientific upheaval in his work compared to previous ideologies.

Following physics, Anaximander focused on biology. Here is where most of his philosophy lies, and it’s a little heartbreaking to say that his ideas here are also the most frivolous and lackluster (in my opinion, at least).

> Anaximander says that the first animals were born in moisture, surrounded by prickly barks. As they grew older, they emerged onto drier parts, the bark burst, and for a short time, they lived a different kind of life. (_Plutarch, On the Scientific Beliefs of the Philosophers_ 9080)

He believed the first creatures to emerge upon this Earth were fish, or very fish-like, and subsequently, humans came out of the bark of these fishes and other animals as the fishes traveled onto land. There is some contention here, for a lot of other researchers believe he speaks of bark as in tree bark, and that animals emerged from trees. Whichever it is, he did believe humans came out of fishes, justifying their killing and eating just as fire consumes its parent.

It’s kind of comical to me how hilariously weird philosophers can be. I am not just talking about humans coming out of fishes, but how he randomly cites fire—just a foolish analogy which serves his purpose, and that’s it. Moving on, the biggest part of Anaximander’s philosophy is the notorious _apeiron_—that is, the infinite, the principle, or the indefinite. Anaximander held belief in a principal element that was unmoving, unending, and that which had no cause or purpose. It simply was and will be.

Here, however, Anaximander contradicts himself. He lends the existence of other elements to thi s apeiron, contradicting its infinity. I had a brutally long debate about this with ChatGPT, and I would like to proudly say that I stood my ground.

But yes. That’s Anaximander’s philosophy. While I was reading _Early Greek Philosophy_, I couldn’t help but notice how similar Greek thought used to be. There is this person conjecturing about various different things, and yet it all just seems to be one family of thought. Separated by years, the Greeks were coalesced through philosophy.
