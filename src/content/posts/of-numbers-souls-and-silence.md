Pythagoras is the only Pre-Socratic philosopher that is spoken of more than the entire collective sum. But in a curious turn of events, Pythagoras wrote down not a word of his philosophy. How odd exactly is that? A man of such renown, and no mood for writing?

Pythagoras is our first non-Miletus-born philosopher, the blessed place this time being Samos, a small island off the coast of modern-day Turkey. He was born in about 570 BC and when he was thirty, he left his homeland and travelled a thousand kilometers to Croton in south Italy, where he set up his political and philosophical life, which the same place from which he was shooed off by the people to Metapontum where he spent his last days. That is pretty much the crux of his life. What was his philosophy? Well, he believed in a principle of things, was an empirical man, had the most devoted followers, was a magician, discovered essential math theorems, and lived multiple past lives. 

In short, Pythagoras is THE PHILOSOPHER. He was the thing of legends, the thing of honor, the thing of science. Socrates, Aristotle, Confucius, Descartes, Kant, Mill simply do not compare. 

Now to explain some of these claims.

Pythagoras was a man of immense prudence. He held belief in the present. He spent his early years learning math and studying the philosophers of the past and by the time he was in Croton, he was an educated human being with an incandescence for life. In the years he spent in Croton, he met other educated individuals, here a little conversation, there a little gesture, slowly he built up a school of thought amongst younger people. This group of people was vehemently revered. Possibly the most revered group of devotees in the history of philosophy. Soon, every man had only two wishes: to learn from Pythagoras or have their sons learn from Pythagoras. Pythagoras never wrote anything down; to reiterate, he believed in the present and wanted to teach through words, he was not bothered by the historicity of his views, his ideas, people’s objections. He wanted to teach and help other human beings. He was a benevolent man, who merely had a love for the universe and a desire to share it. And so his sect came to be known as The Pythagoreans, one of the longest-running schools of thought in Greek philosophy. It is through these following humans that we know anything about the Legend at all.

Now that I have established the secretive school he set up, let’s look at a bitter part of Pythagoras and moreover humanity’s past, in general. Pythagoras was a slave owner. And not just one, he owned many. Being a wealthy (not only in terms of money but in terms of connections) citizen, it was the norm to have workers forever bonded to you. These often skilled people weren’t paid and were treated little more than a pet horse. It is very confusing to me. How such illustrious people come into existence, have knowledge that lasts centuries thereafter, no, *millennia* thereafter, and are somehow unable to recognize the great injustices happening right in front of their eyes. There are countless examples of this in history. It really just makes me wonder, who had a better impact on society, these infinitely popular philosophers or a common man forgotten to history who helped, for example, ameliorate the living conditions of twenty some slaves in his lifetime. Who has really made more of a positive impact? But that thought process, inherently, is extremely superfluous. No amount of thinking, conjecturing, pondering will lend you to quantify “ethical points” a deed can be granted. Nothing of that sort exists, or will ever exist.

Moving on, let us tackle the most well known aspects of this man. His mathematics, which included The Pythagorean Theorem, irrational numbers, figurate numbers, and some of the earliest classification of the number system. There are a few sources that claim these ideas to have pervaded Babylonia, but Pythagoras was the first to prove it. I learned a square plus b square equals c square when I was eleven maybe, and it was just one of those things to learn, you know? But recognizing the story behind it makes me actually appreciate its existence. 

It goes without saying Pythagoras was a smart person to have founded some of the most important math concepts. What follows, isn’t exactly that. Let me take you through a brief tale etched deeply in antiquity. 

There once lived Hermes, the God of messengers and travelers. This God had a son called Aethalides whom he loved dearly. So much so, that he invited Aethalides to choose whatever he wanted in life, with the sole exception being immortality. Aethalides thought about this deeply over supper, ruminating over his best options. Then alas, it finally hit him. He recognized that his physical being wasn’t granted immortality, but, in a sharp turn of events, he realized his metaphysical being was permitted ever lasting existence. Marching up to his father, he asked for him to remember all his memories throughout all his lives. And thus, he foiled a god.

And so the great Aethalides died of old age, his soul forming a new being by the name of Euphorbus. This Euphorbus remembered being Aethalides, he remembered the movement of his soul, understood the cycle of life and had exact memories of his past life. This Euphorbus became a great warrior, and was killed in battle by Menelaus. When Euphorbus died, his soul passed on to Hermotimus, who, yet again remembered everything. Wanting to give proof of the fact, he traveled to Branchidae to the temple of Apollo and pointed straight to Menelaus’ shield. Having lived his life, Hermotimus passed on to give way to Pyrrhus. And this time around, Aethalides chose to be a fisherman. When Pyrrhus died, he became none other than Pythagoras himself. And so goes the tale, heard from the man himself. And so it also goes without saying that Pythagoras believed in metempsychosis (transmigration of soul after death) and was a pious vegetarian since he believed the soul travelled across animals as well. His many lives weren’t the only supernatural things about the philosopher, however. There are recountings such as his biting a poisonous snake that had first bit him, predicting the disaster of a ship, appeared in two places at the same time, having a golden thigh, etc. etc.

Such was the life and death of Pythagoras. In Croton, his school, The Pythagorean Brotherhood rose to such fame, fervor and power that it began pulling political strings all over the city. The tensions boiled to a climax, and the local leaders ended up killing a lot of the school’s members, with Pythagoras only nearly escaping. At least, that’s what most of the sources purport, for it isn’t entirely out of the question that they managed to murder him as well. If he did indeed survive, his last few years in Metapontum are shrouded in ambiguity. If Thales’s mystery was the size of a molecule, Pythagoras in Metapontum is a quark inside one of its atoms. How he lived, what he did, and where he lived, are unknown. Most reliable sources in history coin two things in the same sentence for this: escaped to Metapontum, died there. 

Just goes to show; however eloquent you are for the masses, your fate lies in the hands of a simple few.
