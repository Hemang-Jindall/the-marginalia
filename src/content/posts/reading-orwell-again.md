I did not expect this book to be this short. I have seen it regarded as quite a read many a times, but for me, thus far, it has been sub-par. Maybe it’s just the growing writer in me that finds things easier to write, less valuable, or maybe it’s just my ego trying to levy up a few hinges.

I would like to admit I was wrong. I ignorantly thought I had figured the plot out. I hadn’t outwardly thought of how the book would end, but I just figured some internal conflict might spring up amongst these animals and something they realize that they aren’t too different from human beings. But it looks like everything is all about intelligence, huh? I just finished up chapter 4 and I am guessing the pigs will increase the amount of control they exert on the other animals.

I am guessing boxer or one of the dogs to pipe up about this first or some shit. I guess it’s always a trickle down. Okay, now I would like to talk about the funny aspects such as the way humans are reacting to these animals. It is some alternate reality shite. I like it. It is more fun this way.

It’s interesting to note here that I completely forgot about 1984 for the past days whilst reading this book. Now, of course, you can tell with authors, the writing style, the themes, the dialogue, something eventually gives it away. And honestly, this book doesn’t entirely (maybe because I have forgotten most things from 1984 except of course the plot and characters) so when Napoleon’s character came about, I just figured he’s there to drive some internal conflict.

Turns out, he is smart as fuck (not as smart as Snowball, I think he has probably achieved a lot outside of Animal Farm) and managed to outmaneuver Snowball. And then we see the first few hints of Big Brother, and yeah, he’s a dictator now. It’s funny seeing the same ass tactics being used by Napoleon as Big Brother. Only a matter of time before they abolish free think and criminalize thoughts. It’s interesting seeing authors transform their previous work into more smarter and eloquent versions.

It was a fun read, fucking horrible ending, though. I mean, I would be lying if I said I anticipated animal liberation by the end of the book (It is a piece by George Orwell after all) but I certainly sought a positive ending.

Let me start from the beginning. The book is fairly short and is an easy read like most other Orwell books. It’s easy to get into the world of Animal Farm, however dishonest it may be at times, get accustomed with the characters, the driving plot points and the general causations.

## The Characters

It’s easy to remember the characters, the nuances of each character, and the underlying symbolism associated with each and every character. Although short, Orwell spent a lot of time thinking of unique characters that each represented an emotion in its purest form. Take for example, Boxer, the hardworking and obedient horse. Initially, I took Boxer to be firm, hard and a bit of a bully on the farm. That is, only because of the first few lines where the author introduces the posse.

It is quite evident quite early on that Boxer is nothing of that sort. He may he strong, but he isn’t hard. He is a soft beast, who recognizes his shortcomings and trusts his comrades completely and utterly. So much so that even though he is definitely one of the smarter animals on the farm (compared to the chickens and such) he is the most blinded by his faith to comrade Napoleon. There has never been a character as obedient and hardworking as Boxer.

Let’s talk about Napoleon next, since he is the penultimate Animal Farm character (in my views at least). He is smart, cunning and ruthless. He is Big Brother before Big Brother. He recognizes the hierarchy among the remaining animals after the revolution, pulls some strings and decides to take control over the farm, outmaneuvering Snowball. As he establishes his leadership, he gets rid of unbelievers and nonbelievers starting with the pigs, the smartest of the animals left.

Soon, only devoted pigs remain on the farm, which is when the new Humans begin plotting ways of greater control. The pigs are smart, cunning and also self-indulgent. They maliciously force the animals to satiate their own needs and inevitably force this wheel of suffering to continue.

Other examples I could think of is Benjamin, the unchanging Donkey. At the start of the book, Benjamin is old, weak and doesn’t have much care for the world around him. By the end of the book, Benjamin is older, weaker and still doesn’t have much care for the world around him. He represents the perpetual observer, one who is so nihilistic about life that they stop caring about feeling good things, thinking new thoughts, for the case they might not come true.

He is smart, he knows where revolutions like these lead, he foresees it perhaps even and thus realizes it’s not worth effort. There is one moment, where there is a shift in his character, around the last ten pages of the book, where, even he, the silent observer, the one who never meddles in the affairs of Animal Farm, the one who keeps working, regardless of the other animals, even he succumbs to pain.

Reading the letters written on the cart that was taking away an ill Boxer, he spells out “Horse Slaughterer”, which sends him into a frenzy. Seeing his old friend of twelve years taken away to be killed after a single misfortune, even old Benjamin can’t bear. I believe that is one of the strongest moments in the book. The constant turned into a variable. There are plenty more examples like the cat’s escape, the raven’s subsequent returning, and Squealer’s social engineering.

But instead I would like to talk about the Windmill. To me, the Windmill, like all things in Animal Farm, had held a rational value in the beginning, Electricity. But soon, as the years passed by, the Windmill became a mere reflection of the control demonstrated by the pigs. Soon, the animals forget what they are even building and rebuilding the Windmill for, they only remember that it was important and it would mean a better life for them.

It symbolizes an unwavering goal that has been engraved into the souls of all animals through generations that which all must work towards. The Windmill has been inscribed so deeply in their bloods that the animals soon forget what they’re even fighting for any longer. Oh and yeah, there is the ultimate leader too, Snowball, but alas, we know where that goes.

## The Plot

As most George Orwell books, you know how it ends, but not really how. You already are aware it’s going to be depressing and much worse than what we started with. And of course, Animal Farm is no exception. I think I have recognized major acts as well. Act I - The rebellion. I hadn’t expected the rebellion to come to soon after Old Major’s speech. But anyway, the first act sets up our characters, motivations, and a general sense of hope.

The animals of the farm are finally liberated, work for themselves now, and are generally happier than during Jones’ time. Act II - New Order. Snowball and Napoleon, along with other pigs, being the smartest animals, take charge of the farm. They try to maintain equality and are integrally working towards a better future for their comrades. Act III - Power Struggle.

The dictator wins, causing turmoil amongst the animals, but through their cunning, the pigs keep them second guessing everything they know and have learned. Slowly but surely, they begin the process of oppression. Act IV - The Realization. I thought the book should have been fine, had it ended with the last commandment addition of some animals being more equal, but the author makes it clear for anyone who wasn’t paying attention.

The animals realize (for maybe a brief period only to be honest) that they are eating less than ever, working more than ever, are no longer happy and that the pigs are humans are alike. The ending of the blending of the two species was quite powerful.

I mean that’s pretty much it, right? Animal Farm takes place is an alternate world where animals are smart and humans are dumb as fuck. It is a story about governments, and the people. About oppression, and liberation. About reality, and dreams.
